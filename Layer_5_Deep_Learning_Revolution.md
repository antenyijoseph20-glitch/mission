## AI-018 — 2011: IBM Watson wins Jeopardy!

**Final timeline description**  
IBM's Watson defeated former champions Brad Rutter and Ken Jennings in the televised Jeopardy! contest in February 2011.

**Historical significance**  
Watson demonstrated the potential of large-scale question answering that combined information retrieval, evidence analysis, candidate scoring and confidence estimation.

**Primary research source**  
Ferrucci and colleagues, “Building Watson: An Overview of the DeepQA Project” (2010/2011 research publication).  
- IBM Research publication record: [IBM Research](https://research.ibm.com/)

**Supporting source**  
IBM historical account of Watson's Jeopardy! performance: [IBM](https://www.ibm.com/)

**Editorial safeguards**
- Watson was a specialised question-answering system.
- It was not a modern large language model.
- Explain that DeepQA combined multiple techniques rather than relying on human-like understanding.

## AI-019 — 2012: AlexNet

**Final timeline description**  
AlexNet, a deep convolutional neural network trained using GPUs, achieved a landmark result in the 2012 ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge.

**Historical significance**  
Its performance helped demonstrate the power of deep convolutional networks trained at scale and accelerated interest in deep learning for computer vision.

**Primary source**  
Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton, “ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks” (NeurIPS 2012).  
- [NeurIPS proceedings paper](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2012/hash/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Abstract.html)

**Editorial safeguards**
- The original single CNN reported an 18.9% top-five error in the paper's specified evaluation.
- The often-quoted 15.3% figure refers to the stronger competition ensemble result.
- CNNs existed before AlexNet; it did not invent convolutional neural networks.

## AI-020 — 2014: Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

**Final timeline description**  
Ian Goodfellow and colleagues introduced Generative Adversarial Networks, in which a generator and discriminator are trained in competition to produce increasingly realistic synthetic data.

**Historical significance**  
GANs became an influential framework for generative modelling, especially image synthesis.

**Primary source**  
Ian Goodfellow and colleagues, “Generative Adversarial Nets.”  
- [Original arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661)
- [NeurIPS 2014 proceedings paper](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/5423-generative-adversarial-nets)

**Editorial safeguards**
- The original arXiv submission was in June 2014; the work was presented at NeurIPS 2014.
- GANs were not the first generative models.
- Explain the generator/discriminator competition accurately.

**Verification:** Original arXiv and NeurIPS destinations identified and opened during audit; final application tests pending.

## AI-021 — 2016: AlphaGo defeats Lee Sedol

**Final timeline description**  
DeepMind's AlphaGo defeated Go champion Lee Sedol by four games to one in a five-game match in Seoul in March 2016.

**Historical significance**  
The match demonstrated the strength of combining deep neural networks, reinforcement learning and search in a game with an enormous space of possible moves.

**Primary research source**  
David Silver and colleagues, “Mastering the Game of Go with Deep Neural Networks and Tree Search,” *Nature* (2016).  
- [Nature DOI record](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature16961)

**Supporting source**  
DeepMind's historical account of AlphaGo and its matches: [Google DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/)

**Editorial safeguards**
- AlphaGo had already defeated European champion Fan Hui 5–0 in 2015.
- The Lee Sedol match was not AlphaGo's first professional victory.
- Preserve the 4–1 score.

**Verification:** Nature paper and DeepMind history identified and opened or checked during audit; final application tests pending.

## AI-022 — 2017: Transformer architecture

**Final timeline description**  
Vaswani and colleagues introduced the Transformer architecture, which used attention mechanisms without recurrence or convolution for sequence-transduction tasks.

**Historical significance**  
The architecture enabled more parallelisable training and became foundational to many later large language and multimodal models.

**Primary source**  
Ashish Vaswani and colleagues, “Attention Is All You Need” (NeurIPS 2017).  
- [Original arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)
- [NeurIPS proceedings paper](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/7181-attention-is-all-you-need)

**Editorial safeguards**
- The Transformer did not invent attention mechanisms.
- Its major contribution was demonstrating an architecture based entirely on attention for sequence transduction.
- Do not imply that the 2017 paper itself introduced modern large language models.
