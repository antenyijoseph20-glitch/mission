## 1. Purpose and change control

This document is the master research record for the Interactive AI Timeline Pyramid. It is intended to be reviewed and approved before implementation.

The 29 milestone IDs and their approved titles are permanent. Do not silently rename, remove, reorder, or add milestones. Any proposed change must be documented and approved.

The register distinguishes:
- **Source identified:** a relevant source has been located.
- **Destination opened:** the actual page or document was opened during research.
- **Claim checked:** the source supports the specific historical statement.
- **Application tested:** the link and navigation have passed tests in the finished application.

A source being identified or opened does not mean its link has passed application testing.

## 3. Master milestone register

### Layer 1 — Foundations (1943–1955)

#### AI-001 — 1943: McCulloch–Pitts Artificial Neuron Model

**Final description:** McCulloch and Pitts published a mathematical model showing how simplified neurons could represent logical operations.

**Historical significance:** The work helped establish a mathematical connection between simplified neural activity and computation.

**Primary reference:** McCulloch and Pitts, *A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity* (1943).  
**Reference destination:** [Springer publication record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02478259)  
**Supporting reference:** Carnegie Mellon accessible paper copy — destination to be confirmed in final link audit.

**Editorial safeguards:** Describe this as a simplified logical neuron model, not a modern trainable neural network.

**Status:** Publisher destination identified and opened in research; full text access restricted. Accessible alternative and final application test pending.

#### AI-002 — 1949: Hebbian Learning Theory

**Final description:** Donald Hebb's *The Organization of Behavior* proposed that connections between neurons could strengthen through repeated activity occurring together.

**Historical significance:** Hebb's proposal became an influential conceptual foundation for learning rules in neural networks.

**Primary reference:** Donald O. Hebb, *The Organization of Behavior* (1949).  
**Reference destination:** [Google Books bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books?id=VNetQgAACAAJ)  
**Supporting reference:** Brown (2020), historical review of Hebbian learning — exact destination to be confirmed in final link audit.

**Editorial safeguards:** “Cells that fire together wire together” is a later shorthand, not a direct quotation from Hebb.

**Status:** Bibliographic record identified and opened; full original book access limited. Supporting source and final application test pending.

#### AI-003 — 1950: Alan Turing — Computing Machinery and Intelligence

**Final description:** Turing's paper examined whether machines could think and proposed the Imitation Game as a practical way to investigate machine behaviour.

**Historical significance:** The paper established an enduring framework for discussing machine intelligence and evaluating intelligent behaviour.

**Primary reference:** A. M. Turing, *Computing Machinery and Intelligence*, Mind (1950).  
**Reference destination:** [Oxford Academic publication record](https://academic.oup.com/mind/article/LIX/236/433/986238)  
**Supporting reference:** An accessible alternative is required.

**Editorial safeguards:** The Imitation Game does not prove consciousness or establish that a machine thinks in the same way as a human.

**Status:** Publisher record identified; direct access was unsuccessful in research. Accessible alternative and final application test pending.

#### AI-004 — 1951: SNARC

**Final description:** Marvin Minsky, with the assistance of Dean Edmonds, constructed an early neural-analogue reinforcement machine that was tested on maze-learning problems.

**Historical significance:** SNARC is an early physical demonstration of a machine using reinforcement-like feedback to adjust behaviour.

**Primary reference:** Marvin Minsky's doctoral thesis, *Neural Nets and the Brain Model Problem* (1954), which describes SNARC.  
**Reference destination:** [MIT DSpace thesis record](https://dspace.mit.edu/) — exact item link to be confirmed in final link audit.

**Editorial safeguards:** Do not describe SNARC as a modern reinforcement-learning system.

**Status:** Thesis PDF opened in research; exact stable item URL and final application test pending.

#### AI-005 — 1955: Dartmouth AI Research Proposal

**Final description:** McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester and Shannon proposed a summer 1956 research project to investigate whether aspects of intelligence could be simulated by machines.

**Historical significance:** The proposal helped introduce the term “artificial intelligence” and set out an ambitious research agenda for the Dartmouth summer project.

**Primary reference:** McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester and Shannon, *A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence* (31 August 1955).  
**Reference destination:** [AI Magazine reproduction](https://ojs.aaai.org/aimagazine/) — exact article destination to be confirmed in final link audit.  
**Supporting reference:** Original proposal scan hosted by Stanford — exact item link to be confirmed.

**Editorial safeguards:** Keep the 1955 proposal distinct from the actual 1956 Dartmouth workshop.

**Status:** AI Magazine reproduction and proposal scan opened in research; exact stable destinations and final application test pending.

### Layer 2 — Birth and Early AI Development (1956–1969)

#### AI-006 — 1956: Dartmouth Summer Research Project on AI

**Final description:** Researchers gathered at Dartmouth for a summer project that helped establish artificial intelligence as a distinct research field.

**Historical significance:** The workshop brought together researchers who helped shape early AI research and its agenda.

**Primary reference:** Dartmouth institutional history of the 1956 workshop.  
**Reference destination:** [Dartmouth College](https://home.dartmouth.edu/) — exact historical page destination to be confirmed in final link audit.  
**Supporting reference:** The 1955 proposal, which is evidence of the planned workshop, not a complete record of everything that happened during it.

**Editorial safeguards:** Do not claim that the workshop instantly created modern AI or achieved general intelligence.

**Status:** Dartmouth historical page opened in research; exact stable page URL and final application test pending.

#### AI-007 — 1955–1956: Logic Theorist

**Final description:** Newell, Shaw and Simon developed a program that could discover proofs for theorems in symbolic logic. It was first simulated by hand in December 1955, with its first computer-generated proof in August 1956.

**Historical significance:** Logic Theorist is widely recognised as one of the earliest AI programs and an important demonstration of symbolic problem-solving.

**Primary reference:** Carnegie Mellon archival historical account of the Logic Theorist.  
**Reference destination:** [Carnegie Mellon University](https://www.cmu.edu/) — exact archival item destination to be confirmed in final link audit.

**Editorial safeguards:** Some accounts differ on the exact August day. Use “August 1956” in the public timeline.

**Status:** CMU archival PDF opened in research; exact stable item URL and final application test pending.

#### AI-008 — 1957–1958: Rosenblatt's Perceptron

**Final description:** Frank Rosenblatt developed the perceptron, an early learning system inspired by simplified models of biological neurons.

**Historical significance:** The perceptron became an influential early example of a machine learning system that adjusted its parameters from examples.

**Primary reference:** Frank Rosenblatt, *The Perceptron: A Probabilistic Model for Information Storage and Organization in the Brain* (1958).  
**Reference destination:** [Original paper PDF hosted by Stanford](https://web.stanford.edu/) — exact PDF destination to be confirmed in final link audit.  
**Supporting reference:** Smithsonian record for the Mark I Perceptron — exact item destination to be confirmed.

**Editorial safeguards:** The 1957 date refers to earlier development and reporting; the 1958 paper is a key publication. The basic single-layer perceptron has limitations and is not equivalent to modern deep neural networks.

**Status:** Original paper opened in research; exact stable PDF URL and final application test pending.

#### AI-009 — 1964–1966: ELIZA

**Final description:** Joseph Weizenbaum developed ELIZA at MIT, a program that generated conversational responses using keyword recognition, decomposition rules and response patterns.

**Historical significance:** ELIZA became an influential early demonstration of human-computer conversation and raised questions about how people interpret machine-generated language.

**Primary reference:** Joseph Weizenbaum, *ELIZA — A Computer Program for the Study of Natural Language Communication Between Man and Machine* (1966).  
**Reference destination:** [ACM Digital Library DOI record](https://doi.org/10.1145/365153.365168)  
**Supporting reference:** Accessible copy of the paper hosted by UMBC — exact stable PDF destination to be confirmed.

**Editorial safeguards:** ELIZA should not be described as genuinely understanding language.

**Status:** Institutional bibliographic record opened; ACM full-text access restricted. Accessible alternative identified; final application test pending.

## 4. Cross-cutting historical safeguards

1. Distinguish a research proposal from the event it proposed.
2. Distinguish a paper's submission date, conference presentation date and journal publication date.
3. Do not claim that a milestone invented a field when it actually popularised, advanced or commercialised an existing idea.
4. Distinguish specialised systems from general-purpose AI.
5. Do not treat a model's benchmark result as proof of general intelligence or human-level understanding.
6. For recent developments, separate demonstrated capabilities from announcements, predictions and aspirations.
7. Preserve uncertainty where historical sources disagree; do not manufacture false precision.

## 5. Final link acceptance criteria

A reference is not accepted merely because its title looks relevant.

For each clickable reference in the finished application:
- It must open the intended source, not a generic homepage or unrelated page.
- The destination must support the specific claim attached to it.
- It must not be broken, misleading, redirected to an unrelated destination or unnecessarily duplicated.
- Important references should have an accessible alternative where possible.
- It must work by mouse/touch and keyboard activation.
- Internal links and milestone navigation must lead to the correct item.
- The final application must be checked at desktop, tablet and mobile widths.
